\subsection{Peak and bill reduction}
Tables~\ref{tab:main} and~\ref{tab:service} report six-month means with the range of monthly values, including the split of each bill into energy and demand charges. Uncontrolled charging is dominated by the demand charge, which is 69\% of the bill at both sites. At JPL it also exceeds the 195\,kW site capacity, reaching a mean monthly peak of 296\,kW. The capacity-constrained greedy rules EDF and LLF cap the JPL peak at the capacity limit and cut the bill by 23\%. At Caltech the uncontrolled peak (105\,kW) never reaches the 150\,kW capacity, so EDF and LLF coincide with uncontrolled charging. Greedy rules that are aware only of capacity leave the demand charge untouched whenever capacity does not bind.

Nominal MPC reduces the mean monthly site peak from 296 to 140\,kW at JPL ($-53\%$) and from 105 to 52\,kW at Caltech ($-50\%$), a reduction of 156\,kW and 53\,kW of peak demand at the grid connection (Fig.~\ref{fig:peaks}). The mean monthly bill falls from 6,693 to 4,234\,USD at JPL ($-37\%$) and from 2,355 to 1,537\,USD at Caltech ($-35\%$). The weekday load profiles in Fig.~\ref{fig:profiles} show the mechanism: the controller flattens the morning arrival spike into a plateau and shifts energy away from the 12--18\,h summer on-peak window. The hindsight optimum shows how much is achievable with full information. With every session fully served it costs 3,913\,USD at JPL and 1,291\,USD at Caltech, with mean peaks of 110\,kW and 35\,kW.

\subsection{Realised grid flexibility}\label{sec:res:flex}
Table~\ref{tab:flex} reports the grid-flexibility metrics (Table~\ref{tab:solar} the on-peak and solar metrics) of Section~\ref{sec:metrics} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ldc} the normalised load-duration curves.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Uncontrolled load is extremely peaky.} Its peak-to-average ratio is 10.2 (JPL) and 11.2 (Caltech): the site connection is sized for a load that occurs for a few hours on weekday mornings.
\item \emph{Peak-state control roughly halves that ratio.} PAR falls to 4.8--5.0 (JPL) and 5.6--5.8 (Caltech), the load factor doubles, and the steepest 15-min ramp falls by 31\% (JPL) and 20\% (Caltech). This is achieved by moving 14--17\% (JPL) and 9--14\% (Caltech) of the monthly energy away from the time uncontrolled charging would have drawn it.
\item \emph{Top-up does not erode the flexibility gain.} It leaves the peak unchanged and only raises the load factor further, because it fills existing headroom.
\item \emph{Combining moderate robustness with top-up delivers most of the achievable flexibility.} At $q=0.7$ the peak reduction reaches 62\% (JPL) and 61\% (Caltech), close to the hindsight optimum (63\% and 67\%). The online controller also avoids the large ramps that the hindsight schedule produces at tariff-period boundaries at JPL (99\,kW per 15\,min, against 43\,kW).
\item \emph{LLF delivers peak reduction only through the capacity cap.} It cuts the JPL peak by 34\% exactly to the 195\,kW limit and does nothing at Caltech, where capacity does not bind.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Coincidence with on-site solar}\label{sec:res:pv}
Flattening the morning spike moves charging towards midday (Fig.~\ref{fig:pv}). With an energy-neutral clear-sky PV array, the share of EV energy that is coincident with PV rises from 46.7\% under uncontrolled charging to 53.4\% with MPC + top-up and 60.5\% with MPC + top-up at $q=0.7$ at JPL. At Caltech it rises from 56.1\% to 59.7\% and 64.3\%. The corresponding PV utilisation rises in step (Table~\ref{tab:solar}).

\input{tables/Table5b_solar}
 With a half-size array the gains are smaller but have the same sign; self-sufficiency is 33--38\% for all policies because the array covers less of the load. The controllers were not given any PV signal. Peak-state control therefore aligns workplace charging with solar output as a side effect; Section~\ref{sec:res:pvaware} tests whether a PV-aware objective increases this further.

The same shift has a cost under this tariff. TOU-EV-4 places its highest energy price in the 12--18\,h weekday block. The share of energy delivered in that block rises from 24.7\% to 28.3\% (JPL) and from 26.9\% to 33.9\% (Caltech) with MPC + top-up, and time-of-use energy cost rises by about 5\% relative to uncontrolled charging. Under this legacy tariff the demand-charge signal and the energy-price signal pull in opposite directions, and the demand charge wins because it is 69\% of the uncontrolled bill. Under tariffs with an evening on-peak window, midday charging would be rewarded by both signals.

\subsection{The service cost of acting on declarations}\label{sec:res:diag}
The bill reduction of nominal MPC is not free. It serves 96.0\% (JPL) and 96.3\% (Caltech) of the demanded energy, and only 86.6\% and 87.6\% of vehicles leave with their full energy. The hindsight optimum serves every vehicle at a lower bill, so the shortfall is caused by missing information, not by the tariff or the capacity limit. Valued at the base penalty of 5\,USD/kWh, the penalised cost of nominal MPC (8,468\,USD at JPL, 2,849\,USD at Caltech) is higher than that of LLF at JPL and of uncontrolled charging at Caltech.

Fig.~\ref{fig:unmet} decomposes the undelivered energy into its two causes. The first is vehicles whose declared energy was delivered but was below what they actually accepted: 45\% of the 5,080\,kWh left undelivered at JPL over the six months, and 31\% of 1,575\,kWh at Caltech. The second is vehicles that departed before their declared energy had been delivered (the remaining 55\% and 69\%). Both causes follow from the declaration errors in Fig.~\ref{fig:errors}. Most drivers over-request energy, but a minority under-request, and about a third leave earlier than declared.

\subsection{Calibrated robustness does not close the gap}
Fig.~\ref{fig:sets} shows the calibrated sets. At $q=0.5$ the controller moves deadlines 0.5--1\,h later and scales declared energy down to 0.52--0.65 of its declared value, because the median driver stays longer and receives less than declared. At $q=0.9$ deadlines move 1.3--2.8\,h earlier while the energy multiplier only returns to about 1.0. The upward bias of declared energy is so strong that even the 90th percentile of $\rho$ does not exceed one for claimed sessions.

The consequences are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}. Robust MPC at $q=0.5$ and $0.7$ produces the lowest bills in the study, below the hindsight optimum at JPL for both levels and at Caltech for $q=0.5$. It does so by delivering only 72--89\% of the energy, so the saving is a service cut rather than better scheduling. At $q=0.9$ the energy served returns to the nominal level at JPL (96.3\%) but the earlier deadlines raise the peak to 163\,kW, increasing the bill by 8\% over nominal MPC. At Caltech, energy served at $q=0.9$ (93.6\%) remains below nominal. No protection level improves on nominal MPC in penalised cost at either site. The quantile box can shift and scale targets, but it cannot create energy targets for the minority of vehicles whose needs exceed their declarations, and protecting early leavers requires deadlines so early that the peak rises.

\subsection{Peak-state top-up}
The top-up rule changes the picture (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}). It is best understood as a more efficient use of electrical capacity: the site has already paid, through the demand charge, for a connection of $\hat P^F$ kilowatts this month, and top-up turns otherwise idle paid-for capacity into delivered EV energy. Applied to nominal MPC, it raises energy served to 99.8\% at JPL and 99.2\% at Caltech, and vehicles fully served to 98.9\% and 96.0\%. The monthly peak and demand charge stay exactly those of nominal MPC. The added cost is the energy price of the extra kWh: 142\,USD per month at JPL for about 800\,kWh, and 39\,USD per month at Caltech for about 210\,kWh, roughly 0.18--0.19\,USD/kWh. The bill remains 35\% (JPL) and 33\% (Caltech) below uncontrolled charging, and 15\% below LLF at JPL. The penalised cost falls to 4,610\,USD at JPL and 1,837\,USD at Caltech, the lowest of all online policies at both sites.

Combining top-up with robust targets gives a tunable operating point. At $q=0.7$ the lower targets keep the peak at 112\,kW (JPL) and 41\,kW (Caltech), and the top-up then serves 98.5\% and 97.1\% of energy. The JPL bill is 4,085\,USD, within 4.3\% of the hindsight optimum on average. At $q=0.9$ with top-up, service at JPL reaches 99.9\% (99.6\% of vehicles), but the higher peak costs 20.9\% over the optimum. Operators can therefore choose between a lower bill and fuller service with one parameter, provided top-up is present; without top-up, the same parameter only trades service for cost.

\subsection{Sensitivity}
Table~\ref{tab:sens} reports the sensitivity runs for August and November. Without a demand charge the controllers have no reason to flatten load, and the JPL peak rises to the 195\,kW capacity (Section~\ref{sec:res:tariff}). With twice the demand charge, nominal MPC still serves 96.1\% (JPL) and 95.2\% (Caltech) of energy, whereas robust MPC at $q=0.9$ accepts larger shortfalls (91.2\% and 74.2\% served): its earlier deadlines can then only be met by raising a peak that costs more than the shortfall penalty. With 30- or 90-day calibration windows, bills and energy served change by less than 1\%, and the error distributions are stable (Fig.~\ref{fig:sets}), so the negative result is not an artefact of the window length. Raising the shortfall penalty to 20\,USD/kWh barely changes behaviour (about 96\% served at both sites). The shortfall is not a trade-off the optimiser chooses; it follows from the targets the declarations set, which is why more information or top-up fixes it and re-weighting the objective does not.

\subsection{Tariff design determines whether flexibility is delivered}\label{sec:res:tariff}
Fig.~\ref{fig:tariff} shows how the demand-charge level changes what peak-state control delivers. The sweep uses August and November only, so its uncontrolled peaks (308\,kW at JPL, 116\,kW at Caltech) are the means of those two months and differ from the six-month means in Table~\ref{tab:service} (296\,kW and 105\,kW).
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Without a demand charge, no peak reduction is delivered.} At Caltech the controlled peak equals the uncontrolled peak (116\,kW in August and November). At JPL the only reduction (37\%) is the one the 195\,kW capacity limit imposes on every controlled policy. Without a demand charge, MPC + top-up cannot lower the bill: at JPL it is 1\% more expensive than uncontrolled charging because top-up then fills capacity regardless of price.
\item \emph{A modest demand charge is enough to obtain almost all of the controller's peak reduction.} At 5\,USD/kW-month, about a third of the TOU-EV-4 value, the peak reduction is 55--56\%, essentially the same as at the tariff value (54--56\%) and at twice that value (56--60\%). Beyond this point the demand-charge level mainly scales the monetary saving: 21\% at 5\,USD/kW-month, 37\% at the tariff value and 45--48\% at twice that value.
\item \emph{The online controller's peak saturates while the optimum's does not.} With perfect information the optimum keeps lowering the peak as the demand charge rises, to 68\% reduction at both sites. The online controller's peak is limited by the information available at the time of each decision rather than by the price.
\end{itemize}
The existence of a demand charge, rather than its exact level, therefore decides whether smart charging delivers local peak reduction. Tariff architecture is thus a precondition for realising the flexibility that controllers can technically provide.

\subsection{Out-of-sample validation on held-out 2021 sessions}\label{sec:res:heldout}
Because the top-up rule was designed after the 2019 diagnosis, we test all policies, without any change to code or parameters, on the 2021 extract. Calibration uses the 60 days before each test month (May--June 2021), and the tests cover July and August 2021 (1,605 JPL and 836 Caltech sessions). The period differs from 2019 in two ways that make it a demanding test. Session volumes are lower after the pandemic, and driver behaviour has shifted: the median claimed driver now leaves 0.4--0.5\,h (JPL) and 1.8--2.0\,h (Caltech) earlier than declared, instead of later, and the median delivered-to-declared energy ratio falls to 0.53--0.54 and 0.40--0.41. Table~\ref{tab:heldout} reports the results.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The service gap reappears, larger.} Nominal MPC cuts the bill by 30\% (JPL) and 43\% (Caltech) and the peak by 42\% and 53\%, but serves only 91.0\% and 83.7\% of the energy, with 81.7\% and 75.7\% of vehicles fully served.
\item \emph{Top-up replicates.} MPC + top-up raises energy served to 98.6\% (JPL) and 97.1\% (Caltech) and vehicles fully served to 96.3\% and 95.0\%, with exactly the same peaks as nominal MPC. Bills remain 24\% and 34\% below uncontrolled charging. At JPL its penalised cost (3,580\,USD) is the lowest of all online policies and below LLF and uncontrolled charging (3,737\,USD). At Caltech it is the lowest online value but slightly above uncontrolled charging (1,327 against 1,261\,USD), because the remaining 2.9\% shortfall, valued at 5\,USD/kWh, outweighs the bill saving at this small site.
\item \emph{Calibrated robustness fails more severely.} Under the behavioural shift, the $q=0.9$ deadline shifts move 5--8\,h earlier. The resulting targets can only be met by raising the peak, so the controller accepts shortfalls: energy served falls to 56\% (JPL) and 35\% (Caltech), and top-up recovers only part of it. At $q=0.7$ with top-up, JPL still reaches 97.3\% served, but Caltech only 84.2\%.
\end{itemize}
The held-out test therefore confirms the two central findings out of sample. Peak-state control with top-up retains its peak and bill reductions with near-complete service, and quantile robustness calibrated on declarations is fragile once driver behaviour drifts.

\subsection{PV-aware control: from coincidence to self-consumption}\label{sec:res:pvaware}
Table~\ref{tab:pv} reports the PV scenario of Section~\ref{sec:pvaware}. With an energy-neutral PV array behind the meter, uncontrolled charging still sets an import peak of 282\,kW at JPL and 79\,kW at Caltech, because the morning arrival spike precedes most of the solar output.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{At JPL, PV awareness adds a large second layer of flexibility.} PV-unaware MPC + top-up lowers the import peak to 128\,kW. Making the controller PV-aware lowers it further to 73\,kW, 26\% of the uncontrolled value. The bill falls by another 32\% (from 2,777 to 1,878\,USD), PV self-sufficiency rises from 50.7\% to 58.0\%, and 99.2\% of energy is still served. With moderate robustness ($q=0.7$) the import peak reaches 46\,kW and self-sufficiency 64.4\%, close to the PV-aware optimum (43\,kW; 66.5\%), at 97.0\% energy served.
\item \emph{At Caltech, PV awareness alone adds nothing.} The PV-aware and PV-unaware top-up controllers give almost the same import peak (26 and 25\,kW) and self-sufficiency (about 60\%), because Caltech's later arrivals already coincide with solar output. Only the robust variant moves further (16\,kW; 63.6\%), and the optimum shows that 72.7\% self-sufficiency would be attainable with perfect information.
\end{itemize}
The value of renewable-aware control is therefore site-specific. It is largest where arrivals precede solar output. As with the demand charge, the gap to the optimum is limited by the information drivers provide rather than by the solar resource.

\subsection{Transformer hosting limits: deliverable flexibility}\label{sec:res:xfmr}
Table~\ref{tab:xfmr} reports the transformer scenarios of Section~\ref{sec:xfmr}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Uncontrolled charging overloads.} On a transformer rated at 50\% of its uncontrolled peak, uncontrolled charging reaches 2.1 (JPL) and 2.2 (Caltech) times the rating, for 38 and 32 hours per month. At a 35\% rating the loading reaches about 3 per unit for 54--57 hours per month, with 4,427\,kWh (JPL) and 1,141\,kWh (Caltech) of monthly energy above the rating.
\item \emph{Within a 50\% rating, controlled charging delivers almost full service.} MPC + top-up stays at 0.92 (JPL) and 0.98 (Caltech) of the rating while serving 99.8\% and 99.0\% of energy, so its demand-charge-driven peak reduction already satisfies the network limit with headroom.
\item \emph{At a 35\% rating, the limit binds for every online policy.} MPC + top-up then serves 98.4\% (JPL) and 96.6\% (Caltech) of energy, and LLF serves 98.3\% and 97.2\%. The hindsight optimum still serves 100\% and 99.7\% within the same rating. The limit is therefore physically compatible with full service; online controllers fall short because they lack information, not capacity. Under a tight network limit the simple LLF rule is as good as MPC on service, because the binding constraint is capacity rather than cost.
\end{itemize}
For a distribution operator, these results translate site-level control into a deliverable quantity. A workplace fleet that would load its transformer to two or three times its rating can be held within the rating, with only 0--3\% of energy unserved, by controllers that need nothing beyond plug-in events and meter readings.

\subsection{Tariff archetypes}\label{sec:res:arch}
Table~\ref{tab:arch} compares the historical TOU-EV-4 energy prices with the two stylised archetypes, keeping the same demand charge.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Peak reduction is unchanged.} MPC + top-up reduces the peak by 56\% (JPL) and 54\% (Caltech) under all three energy-price structures, with 99.8\% and 99.0\% of energy served. This confirms that the demand charge, not the energy-price structure, drives peak reduction.
\item \emph{The energy-price conflict disappears.} Under TOU-EV-4, flattening raises the energy cost by 6.1\% (JPL) and 5.4\% (Caltech) because it moves charging into the midday on-peak block. Under the evening-peak archetype the change is +2.6\% and $-0.7$\%, and under a flat price it is $-0.2$\% and $-0.9$\%.
\item \emph{Savings are therefore larger.} The total bill saving rises from 37\% under TOU-EV-4 to 40--41\% under both archetypes.
\end{itemize}
Tariffs that combine a demand charge with an evening peak or a flat energy price, as common outside California, align the operator's incentive with both local peak reduction and midday solar absorption.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
%======================================================================
\subsection{From technical to grid-usable flexibility}
The results distinguish the technical flexibility of workplace charging, bounded by parking duration and connected capacity, from the flexibility that is actually usable by the grid. Grid-usable flexibility is what a controller can deliver while keeping drivers served, and what a network operator can rely on. Three outcomes need to be evaluated jointly: charging service to drivers (energy and vehicles fully served), operator cost (the bill), and grid flexibility delivered at the connection point (peak, load factor and ramps). Uncontrolled charging maximises service and minimises flexibility. Nominal and robust MPC trade service for cost and flexibility. Robust MPC at low $q$ looks best on cost and peak alone precisely because it withholds energy. Any assessment that reports only one or two of these dimensions can rank policies wrongly. MPC + top-up is the only online policy that is close to the frontier on all three at once, and combining it with moderate robustness moves further along the flexibility axis at a small service cost.

\subsection{Information quality, not optimisation, limits realisable flexibility}
The negative result on calibrated robustness is an empirical energy-management finding rather than a failure of the optimisation method. Better mathematical protection cannot compensate for structurally poor information. Declared energy is an upper bound for most drivers and a lower bound for a few, and declared departure is late for about a third. Constraint-wise robust counterparts over boxes, whether built from quantiles as here or from more elaborate data-driven sets \cite{Bertsimas2018DataDriven,Esfahani2018Wasserstein}, can shift and scale targets but cannot add energy that no target requests, and they pay for early-leaver protection with higher peaks. What works is recourse to realised information: the observation that a vehicle is still drawing current, used by top-up. This is consistent with the caution against over-conservative robust solutions \cite{Bertsimas2004Price,Gorissen2015Guide}. It also suggests that per-driver prediction \cite{Lee2019ACNData,Wang2016Predictive,Kreft2024Predictability} and incentives for accurate declarations are the most promising levers for realising more of the flexibility that parking durations imply.

\subsection{Implications for energy-system actors}
\paragraph{Charging-site operators} Demand-charge-aware control is worth roughly a third of the electricity bill and half of the site peak. Greedy capacity-constrained rules capture peak reduction only where capacity binds. Any headroom below the month's realised peak should be filled with top-up, which costs only the energy price. Operators should always report energy served alongside savings.

\paragraph{Aggregators and utilities} The site peak of a controlled workplace fleet is determined in the first days of the billing month and is then defended. The realised month-to-date peak $\hat P^F(\tau)$ is therefore a natural, verifiable firm-capacity limit that a site can offer to a distribution operator, an aggregator or a virtual power plant, while the headroom below it remains available for flexible energy. The peak-state formulation extends directly to such arrangements by replacing the site peak with a contracted import limit or a time-varying network signal. Our results indicate that an aggregator should expect a controlled workplace fleet to deliver roughly half of its uncontrolled peak as firm reduction. The transformer scenarios show that this is enough to keep a transformer rated at half the uncontrolled peak within its rating with near-complete service. With behind-the-meter PV and PV-aware control at a site like JPL, the import peak can fall to about a quarter of its uncontrolled value. Deeper reductions, up to the 63--68\% achieved in hindsight, require better information than drivers' declarations.

\paragraph{Tariff designers} The tariff sweep shows that the presence of a demand charge, not its level, determines whether any local peak reduction is delivered. Commercial EV rates that suspend demand charges \cite{CPUCEVRates} remove the operator's incentive to flatten workplace load. A small demand charge, or an equivalent subscription or import-limit product, is sufficient to unlock the technically available peak reduction. The tariff archetypes show that moving the energy on-peak window to the evening, or using a flat energy price, removes the conflict between energy-price and demand-charge signals observed under TOU-EV-4. Peak reduction is unchanged under every energy-price structure, and the saving rises from 37\% to 40--41\%.

\subsection{Deployment and scalability}
In real time, the controller needs each vehicle's plug-in event and, where available, its declaration; a per-port signal showing whether the vehicle is still drawing current; the site meter reading, to maintain the month-to-date peak; and the tariff. All of these are available in networked charging systems of the ACN type \cite{Lee2021ACN}. Rates are dispatched to the charging stations as pilot signals every control period. Each re-solve is a sparse LP whose size grows linearly with the number of connected vehicles and the remaining horizon; mean solve times were 0.013\,s for JPL's 52 ports and 0.005\,s at Caltech on a single core, and the monthly hindsight LP took 8.1\,s and 2.3\,s. Sites with hundreds of ports, or aggregators controlling many sites, remain well within the minute-scale control loops of deployed systems. The robust variant has exactly the size of the nominal one. Top-up is a sorting step that requires no optimisation. Calibration requires only a rolling store of past sessions with their declarations.

\subsection{Distribution-network constraints}
Section~\ref{sec:res:xfmr} adds the transformer as a hard import limit and quantifies overloads under uncontrolled charging and the service deliverable within the limit. This is a static capacity representation. It does not model transformer thermal dynamics or ageing, feeder voltage, three-phase imbalance or power flow, all of which are present in real workplace installations \cite{Lee2021ACN,Lee2021ACNSim} and in distribution networks \cite{ClementNyns2010,deHoog2015Network,Gong2012Transformer}. The peak-state structure is compatible with richer models. Per-phase or per-transformer capacity constraints can be added to the LP as in \cite{Lee2021ACN,deHoog2015Network}, transformer or feeder peaks can be tracked as additional peak states with their own charges or limits, and the top-up rule then fills headroom below the tightest of these limits.

\subsection{Generalisability}
Three elements of the findings are structural and should transfer to other sites and tariffs. These are the monthly peak-state logic, under which capacity paid for early in the month is free thereafter; the gap between declared and realised behaviour, documented in other datasets \cite{Kreft2024Predictability,Flammini2019Stats}; and the opportunity to re-use paid-for capacity through top-up. The legacy TOU-EV-4 price windows, the commuting patterns of two southern Californian campuses, Level-2 charger power and site capacities, and the 2019 period are context-specific. The magnitudes reported here (half the peak, a third of the bill) should therefore be re-estimated elsewhere. The qualitative ordering of policies and the mechanism behind the robustness failure are expected to hold wherever declarations are biased and wide, as the held-out 2021 test and the tariff archetypes already indicate.

\subsection{Limitations}
Vehicles are modelled as ideal loads that accept energy at up to $\bar r_i$ until full. Battery tapering is not represented, and $\bar r_i$ is estimated because the extract lacks the done-charging time. The energy delivered by the ACN in 2019 is used as each vehicle's demand and may itself be truncated by the controller in place at the time. The PV scenarios use clear-sky irradiance and a horizontal array, because measured irradiance for the sites was not accessible to us; they overstate solar output on cloudy days, and surplus PV is curtailed without export credit. Transformer limits are static, and holidays and customer charges are omitted. The study covers two sites, six test months and a two-month held-out period, under one historical tariff and two stylised archetypes. The top-up rule was designed after the 2019 diagnosis and validated on held-out 2021 sessions, but only for two months, and it was not included in the sensitivity runs.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
%======================================================================
The flexibility of workplace EV charging is not determined only by how long cars are parked or how much charging capacity is connected. On 13,623 real sessions from two California workplaces, peak-state control that prices only increments above the month-to-date peak roughly halved the site peak (from 296 to 140\,kW and from 105 to 52\,kW). Acting on driver declarations, however, left about one vehicle in eight short of its energy, and robust counterparts calibrated to empirical declaration errors did not close this gap. Better mathematical protection could not compensate for structurally poor information. Re-using capacity already billed in the month restored service to above 99\% without a new peak, and did so again on held-out 2021 sessions.

The same controllers convert technical flexibility into grid-usable flexibility. They held a transformer rated at half the uncontrolled peak within its rating with near-complete service, where uncontrolled charging overloaded it by a factor of two. Where arrivals precede solar output, PV-aware control lowered the import peak to about a quarter of its uncontrolled value. The presence of a demand charge, not its level or the energy-price structure, decided whether any peak reduction was delivered.

Information quality and tariff design therefore jointly determine how much of the technical flexibility of workplace charging can be realised without degrading mobility service. Future work should validate the controllers over longer periods and at other sites, replace population-level uncertainty sets with per-driver predictions and declaration incentives, and use measured irradiance. It should also add transformer thermal, feeder voltage and three-phase models as additional peak states, and test the realised peak as a contracted flexibility product in aggregator and virtual-power-plant settings.
